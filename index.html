LabelLens

Turns an English pharmacy label into clear instructions in the patient's own language, with a picture schedule and audio. It never translates freely: it reads the label into fixed parts and rebuilds it from checked phrases, and it says “ask your pharmacist” instead of guessing.

Why not just use Google Translate?

Free translation gets medical instructions wrong, and worst in the languages Western Sydney speaks. Accuracy of Google Translate on emergency discharge instructions:

Spanish94%
Chinese82%
Farsi67.5%
Armenian55%

Source: Taira et al., J Gen Intern Med, 2021.

How LabelLens stays safe

It breaks the label into dose, how often, when, food and warnings. Each part is shown with a phrase checked in advance, so nothing is invented. Anything it cannot read is shown in English with “ask your pharmacist”. Complex labels, such as reducing doses or weekly medicines, are refused on purpose.

What is in this prototype

14 languages: the 13 most spoken in Western and South Western Sydney plus Persian, and an “Other language” option with pictures and free interpreter details. It covers the most common dose patterns on Australian labels, with audio where the device has a voice and a check question. Translations are drafts until reviewed by native speakers.
